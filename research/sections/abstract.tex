\begin{abstract}
Automated forecasting systems that train many candidate models must decide which one to serve, and a common design is to rank candidates by a weighted sum of several criteria---accuracy, forecast stability, and computational cost---on the reasoning that a single error metric is an incomplete basis for practical model selection. This paper subjects one such composite score to a held-out evaluation and reports that it does not work. We benchmark a six-term composite score, deployed in a nineteen-model retail forecasting system, against 487 series drawn from five aggregation levels of the M5 competition hierarchy, using a test window never exposed to model selection and reporting mean absolute scaled error. The composite score is outperformed by ranking on cross-validated RMSE alone at every aggregation level, decisively so where the comparison is adequately powered ($p=2.1\times10^{-7}$ at store$\times$department, $p=3.6\times10^{-6}$ at item level). We trace the cause and find it structural rather than a poor choice of weights: each criterion is normalized by its own Tukey fence, which bounds the denominator but not the resulting ratio, so training time---spanning six orders of magnitude across the model registry---produces normalized values reaching~43 while RMSE, spanning a factor of~1.2, remains within~$[0.5, 1.0]$. A term carrying a nominal weight of~0.10 consequently determines between 63\% and 88\% of all selections, and the models it excludes are precisely those that generalize best. Neither re-weighting nor a corrected normalization recovers the gap. Re-expressing the same cost requirement as a constraint rather than a scoring term does: selecting the most accurate model that trains within a fixed budget beats single-metric selection by approximately 5\% MASE on fine-grained series (five of five folds, 95\% confidence interval excluding zero, stable across random seeds) while consuming an order of magnitude less computation, and degenerates gracefully to unconstrained selection at coarse aggregation where no constraint is warranted. We further show that selecting a scoring configuration on the same data used to evaluate it inflates apparent performance by 0.12--0.20 MASE, exceeding every effect reported here, and that nested cross-validation over series is therefore necessary rather than optional. We conclude that heterogeneous criteria differing by orders of magnitude in scale should not be combined additively, and that cost belongs in the constraint set rather than the objective.
\end{abstract}
