\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper evaluated a multi-criteria composite score for automated forecasting model selection against held-out data and found that it does not perform its intended function. Across 487 series drawn from five aggregation levels of the M5 hierarchy, ranking candidate models by cross-validated RMSE alone produced lower realized error than the six-term composite at every level, decisively so where the comparison was adequately powered ($p=2.1\times10^{-7}$ and $p=3.6\times10^{-6}$ at two independent levels). At its worst the composite was statistically indistinguishable from a seasonal-naive forecast.

The cause is structural rather than a matter of weight calibration. Normalizing each criterion by its own Tukey fence bounds the denominator but not the resulting ratio, so a training-time term spanning six orders of magnitude produced normalized values reaching~43 while accuracy terms, spanning a factor of~1.2, remained near unity. A criterion carrying a nominal weight of~0.10 consequently governed between 63\% and 88\% of selections, systematically excluding the classical statistical models that generalized best. Correcting the normalization so that effective influence matches nominal weight is necessary for the score to mean what it claims, but it does not make the score competitive: no weighted-sum configuration examined outperformed single-metric ranking.

Re-expressing the same requirement as a constraint does. Selecting the most accurate model that trains within a fixed budget outperformed single-metric selection by approximately 0.040 MASE at the two finest aggregation levels, winning in all five folds with confidence intervals excluding zero and replicating across random seeds, while consuming roughly an order of magnitude less computation. Under nested selection over series, a budget-constrained policy was chosen at every level and no weighted-sum configuration was chosen at either fine-grained level. Where no constraint was warranted the procedure selected a budget exceeding every candidate's cost, degenerating correctly to unconstrained selection.

A secondary finding concerns evaluation methodology. Selecting a scoring configuration on the same series used to report it inflates apparent performance by 0.119 to 0.204 MASE---three to five times any effect measured here---so nested cross-validation over series is a precondition for claims of this kind rather than a refinement of them. The same bias arose at four distinct levels of abstraction during this work, which suggests it is a structural hazard of automated selection systems rather than an incidental oversight.

The contribution is therefore threefold: a negative result establishing that additive combination of heterogeneous criteria fails when their effective ranges differ by orders of magnitude, with the mechanism identified and quantified; a validated alternative in which cost is expressed as a constraint on the candidate set rather than a term in the objective; and an independent empirical confirmation via forecast combination that the 0.040 MASE gain from the constrained formulation measures the value of correct selection in a regime where combination cannot compensate for it. The system evaluated here was modified accordingly, replacing the composite score with accuracy-based ranking and an optional, disabled-by-default training-time budget. We suggest that automated machine learning systems combining quality and resource criteria in a single scalar objective should verify, rather than assume, that the resulting influence matches the intended one.
