\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Experimental Scope and Completion}
The evaluation covers 487 series drawn from five aggregation levels of the M5 hierarchy (Table~\ref{tab:levels}). Two levels are structurally small: M5 contains exactly ten stores and seven departments, so levels~3 and~5 exhaust their populations rather than sample them and can never be enlarged. Results at those levels are therefore reported descriptively and no significance is claimed from them. Levels~9, 10, and~12 supply 70, 200, and 200 series respectively and carry the statistical weight of the study.

\input{tables/levels.tex}

Every series contains 1{,}941 daily observations before the held-out window is removed, which places all of them above the system's 500-observation minimum for deep learning at daily frequency. All nineteen models therefore trained on every series, the first configuration in which the deep learning family was exercised rather than gated out. At level~9, 1{,}330 of 1{,}330 model fits completed without failure or timeout.

\subsection{Composite Scoring Versus Single-Metric Ranking}
Table~\ref{tab:cross-level} reports the central comparison. At every aggregation level, ranking candidates on a single cross-validated accuracy metric produces lower realized held-out error than the six-term composite score of Eq.~\eqref{eq:composite-score}. Where the comparison is adequately powered the margin is decisive: against cross-validated RMSE the composite loses 50 of 61 decided series at level~9 ($p_{\text{holm}}=2.1\times10^{-7}$) and 117 of 169 at level~10 ($p_{\text{holm}}=3.6\times10^{-6}$). At level~12 the direction is unchanged but the effect attenuates to marginal significance ($p_{\text{holm}}=0.079$), which we report rather than omit; the policy spread compresses substantially at item--store granularity, where every method converges toward seasonal-naive performance.

\input{tables/cross_level.tex}

The result does not depend on which accuracy metric serves as the baseline. Selection on cross-validated MAE is comparable to, and by median marginally better than, selection on RMSE at every level; the composite loses to both. We retain RMSE as the reference baseline throughout because it is the conventional choice and because it is the metric the composite itself weights most heavily, but the finding should be read as concerning single-metric selection generally rather than RMSE in particular.

The composite is additionally not distinguishable from a seasonal-naive forecast at level~9 ($p_{\text{holm}}=0.149$) and is outperformed by it outright at level~12 (median 0.774 against 0.766). At no level does it beat the practice of always using one fixed model such as Prophet or ARIMA.

\subsection{Which Term Actually Governs Selection}
The composite assigns 80\% of its nominal weight to accuracy terms (0.40 RMSE, 0.20 MAE, 0.20 MAPE) and only 0.10 to computational speed. Its behavior does not follow that intent. Decomposing each model's score into per-term contributions and measuring, for every series, the spread each term induces across the candidate set gives the effective influence reported in Table~\ref{tab:term-dominance}. The speed term accounts for between 63\% and 88\% of all discriminating power, while RMSE---carrying four times the nominal weight---accounts for between 3.5\% and 7.1\%.

\input{tables/term_dominance.tex}

The cause is visible in the normalized ranges. On a representative level-9 series, normalized RMSE spans $[0.479, 1.032]$ while normalized speed spans $[3.6\times10^{-5}, 43.55]$, a ratio of 78.8 between the two spreads. Each term is divided by its own Tukey fence, $Q_3 + 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}$, which bounds the \emph{denominator} but places no bound on the resulting ratio. Training time across the registry ranges from approximately $5\times10^{-5}$~s for a moving average to 118~s for a grid-searched SARIMA---six orders of magnitude---whereas cross-validated RMSE across the same candidates differs by a factor of roughly 1.2. Because only three of nineteen models are slow, both quartiles of the training-time distribution fall among the fast models, the interquartile range is small, and the fence lands far below the actual cost of the expensive candidates.

This is not a subtle preference: the composite never selects SARIMA or SARIMAX at level~9, in any of the 70 series, despite those two models being the choice of RMSE-based selection in 57 of them and appearing among the oracle's selections 21 times. Isolating why, term by term and series by series, the speed term is the single largest contributor to their exclusion in 100\% of series, with a mean contribution gap of $+3.52$ against a countervailing RMSE advantage of only $-0.012$---a ratio of roughly 300 to~1.

\subsection{Correcting the Normalization}
Two defects follow from the analysis above: the fence bounds denominators rather than ratios, and $R^2$ is the one term never normalized at all, entering the sum as a raw signed quantity alongside five scaled ones. We evaluated two corrections against the shipped scheme, holding weights, models, forecasts, and held-out data fixed so that only the scaling varies. The \emph{clipped} scheme caps every fence-normalized term at 1.0 and places $R^2$ on the same footing; the \emph{balanced} scheme additionally rescales each capped term across the candidate set so that every criterion spans exactly $[0,1]$ and effective influence equals nominal weight by construction.

Both corrections behave exactly as designed---under \emph{balanced}, measured effective shares match nominal weight shares to six decimal places---and neither materially improves realized accuracy. At level~9 the sequence is 1.324, 1.326, and 1.311 MASE for shipped, clipped, and balanced respectively, with the balanced scheme changing only 15 of 70 selections (Wilcoxon $p=0.21$). Correcting the scaling is necessary for the score to mean what it claims, but it is not sufficient to make the score competitive.

\subsection{Configuration Selection Under Nested Cross-Validation}
Because no single correction sufficed, we compared a family of candidate scoring configurations: the shipped weights under both normalizations, three leave-one-criterion-out variants, an accuracy-only configuration discarding MAPE, speed, and stability, and---critically---twelve budget-constrained policies of the form ``the most accurate model that trains within $T$ seconds.'' Selecting among these on all available series and reporting the winner would repeat precisely the error this study exists to characterize, so configurations are chosen within a five-fold partition over \emph{series} and scored only on the fold withheld from that choice.

\input{tables/config_selection.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:config-selection} reports the outcome. A budget-constrained policy is selected at every level, and no weighted-sum configuration is ever selected at levels~10 or~12. At the two finest levels the selected policy beats single-metric ranking by approximately 0.040 MASE, winning in all five folds with a 95\% confidence interval excluding zero; at level~12 this replicates across three random fold assignments (margins $+0.040$, $+0.037$, $+0.040$), with the same 1.5-second budget chosen in fourteen of fifteen folds. At level~9 the procedure instead selects a 30-second budget, which---since no candidate there costs more than 13.6~s---is unconstrained selection under another name, yielding a margin of exactly zero in four of five folds. The method thus declines to impose a constraint where none helps.

\subsection{Cost as a Constraint Rather Than a Penalty}
Table~\ref{tab:frontier} reports realized error as a function of the training-time budget. The relationship is non-monotone in held-out accuracy while remaining monotone in the selection objective: mean cross-validated RMSE of the selected model decreases uniformly as the budget relaxes, exactly as it must, yet realized MASE reaches a minimum at 1.5--2~seconds and worsens thereafter. Admitting the models with the lowest cross-validated error actively degrades out-of-sample performance. A compute budget functions here as regularization against selecting models that overfit their validation folds---an effect present at levels~10 and~12 and absent at level~9, consistent with finer-grained series being shorter, noisier, and more susceptible to such overfitting.

\input{tables/frontier.tex}

Compared at matched cost, the constrained formulation dominates the composite outright. Figures in this subsection are means, matching the frontier of Table~\ref{tab:frontier}, rather than the medians of Table~\ref{tab:cross-level}. At level~12 the composite achieves 0.769 mean MASE while consuming 4.00~seconds of training time for the model it selects; a 2-second budget achieves 0.698 at 0.93~seconds. The constrained policy is simultaneously 9\% more accurate and 77\% cheaper. It also beats unconstrained single-metric selection, which requires 10.84~seconds to reach 0.735. The same pattern holds at level~10 (0.782 at 0.93~s against the composite's 0.898 at 2.82~s and single-metric's 0.826 at 7.07~s).

\subsection{The Cost of Selecting In Sample}
Across all three powered levels, the configuration that appears best when scored on every series outperforms the honest nested estimate by 0.119 to 0.204 MASE. This optimism exceeds every treatment effect reported in this paper by roughly a factor of three to five. Had configurations been chosen and reported on the same series---the natural procedure absent explicit precautions---the resulting claims would have been artifacts of that choice rather than measurements of it.

\subsection{Deep Learning Model Performance}
This is the first configuration in which all seven deep learning architectures trained rather than being withheld by the row-count gate, every series having 1{,}913 training observations against a 500-observation threshold. They are nonetheless almost entirely absent from the oracle's selections at levels~9 and~10, which are dominated by Prophet, SARIMAX, Holt--Winters, and SARIMA. Their appearance in composite-score selections at level~12 (85 of 200 selections) reflects not accuracy but cost: shorter, sparser series train quickly, so the speed term ceases to exclude them. The gating policy's premise---that deep learning requires substantially more data than these datasets provide to be competitive---is supported by its own out-of-sample results.
