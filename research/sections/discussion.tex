\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Additive Combination Fails for Heterogeneous Criteria}
The composite score's failure is not attributable to a poor choice of weights, and this distinction matters because it determines what would repair it. A weighted sum expresses a fixed exchange rate between criteria: a unit of normalized error is declared equivalent to some quantity of normalized cost. That exchange rate is only meaningful if the normalized quantities are commensurable, and normalizing by a robust denominator does not make them so. The Tukey fence bounds what is divided \emph{by}; it says nothing about the magnitude of the resulting ratio.

Across this model registry, cross-validated RMSE varies between candidates by roughly 20\%, because a family of reasonable forecasting methods applied to the same series produces broadly similar accuracy. Training time varies by six orders of magnitude, because the methods differ not in degree but in kind---a moving average performs a handful of arithmetic operations while a grid-searched SARIMA fits hundreds of candidate models. After division by their respective fences, the accuracy terms occupy a narrow band near unity while the cost term ranges over two decades. The stated 4:1 preference for accuracy over speed operates in practice as roughly 1:25 against it.

This generalizes beyond the specific system studied here. Any additive multi-criteria score combining an accuracy measure with a resource measure is exposed to the same failure whenever the resource distribution is heavy-tailed, which it typically is: computational cost across algorithm families is naturally multiplicative, while accuracy among viable algorithms is naturally bounded. The defect is not that the weights were chosen badly but that no fixed weight vector can be correct when the terms' effective ranges differ by two orders of magnitude and vary with the dataset.

\subsection{Constraints Rather Than Penalties}
Expressing a resource requirement as a constraint avoids the problem entirely, because a budget is absolute where a penalty is relative. Under the composite score, a model taking two seconds is penalized severely when its competitors are instantaneous and barely at all when they are slow---the same model receives different treatment depending on the company it keeps. A budget states a requirement the deployment actually has, admits every candidate meeting it, and optimizes accuracy exactly within that set. The resulting accuracy--latency frontier is directly interpretable by an operator choosing an operating point, which an implied exchange rate is not.

The empirical result is stronger than the argument requires. At the two finest aggregation levels the constrained formulation is not merely a better articulation of the same trade-off; it dominates the composite on both axes simultaneously, achieving lower error at lower cost, and it also outperforms unconstrained single-metric selection. The last of these was unexpected and is the study's most substantive positive finding.

\subsection{Compute Budgets as Regularization}
The frontier's non-monotonicity in realized error, against strict monotonicity in the selection objective, admits a clean interpretation. Relaxing the budget can only enlarge the candidate set, so the minimum achievable cross-validated error can only fall---and it does, uniformly. That held-out error nevertheless rises past a threshold means the additional candidates admitted at higher budgets achieve their lower validation error partly by fitting the validation folds rather than the underlying process. Excluding them by cost is a form of capacity control that happens to correlate with the mechanism producing the overfitting: the expensive models in this registry are expensive precisely because they search large hyperparameter spaces, and search over a validation criterion is the standard route to overfitting it.

That the effect is present at levels~10 and~12 and absent at level~9 supports rather than complicates this reading. Finer aggregation yields sparser, noisier series in which validation-fold error is a weaker estimate of generalization error, so selection on that estimate is more easily corrupted and a constraint that limits search has more to correct. Where series are well-aggregated and validation error is reliable, the procedure correctly declines to constrain, selecting a budget exceeding every candidate's cost.

\subsection{Selection Bias as a Recurring Hazard}
The same error appeared four times in the course of this work at successively higher levels of abstraction: selecting a model on cross-validation folds and reporting that fold error; tuning score weights on the evaluation set; selecting a scoring configuration on all available series; and, finally, reading an optimal budget off a frontier computed over the same series used to report it. Each instance is individually easy to overlook, and each inflates the reported result in the same direction.

The measured magnitude justifies the concern quantitatively rather than in principle. In-sample configuration selection is optimistic by 0.119 to 0.204 MASE---between three and five times every treatment effect reported in this paper. A study of this design that omitted nesting would produce claims determined by that bias rather than by the interventions it purports to measure. We therefore report the performance of the selection \emph{procedure} rather than of any selected artifact, and treat budgets as candidates subject to the same discipline as scoring configurations.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
Several considerations bound the interpretation of these results. The evaluation uses a single forecast origin per series at a fixed 28-step horizon; rolling-origin evaluation across multiple horizons would strengthen the estimates, though the direction of the principal comparison is consistent across five aggregation levels and three random fold assignments. All results derive from one dataset family, and while M5 is the standard retail forecasting benchmark, the specific magnitudes---particularly the 1.5--2-second budget optimum---should not be assumed transferable to registries with different cost profiles. The mechanism, being a statement about the relative spread of heterogeneous criteria, is expected to transfer more readily than the constants.

The effect sizes at level~10 warrant explicit caution: a margin of 0.040 MASE with a confidence interval spanning $[0.020, 0.061]$ is real but modest, and the interval derives from five folds. The negative result---that the composite score loses to single-metric ranking---rests on considerably firmer ground, with $p$-values of $10^{-6}$ or smaller at two independent levels.

Finally, the exogenous-feature capability described in Section~\ref{sec:proposed} is weakly exercised by this evaluation. M5 supplies no explicit promotion indicator, and a markdown proxy derived from price movements fires on only 21\% of series at level~9 and 45\% at level~12, with many items exhibiting monotonically non-decreasing prices. Claims regarding exogenous integration should be read as describing implemented capability rather than validated benefit.

\subsection{Implications for System Design}
The practical consequence for the system studied here was to replace the composite score with ranking on cross-validated RMSE, with an optional training-time budget disabled by default. The default is accuracy-only rather than budget-constrained because the useful budget proved dataset-dependent---beneficial at fine granularity, harmful at coarse---and cannot be determined from information available at request time. Accuracy-only ranking is the configuration that measured no worse than the alternative at every level examined, which makes it the appropriate default; a budget is exposed for deployments where latency is a genuine requirement rather than an inferred preference.

More broadly, the finding argues against a common pattern in automated machine learning systems, in which several desirable properties are folded into a single scalar objective for convenience of implementation. Where the criteria are commensurable, that is reasonable. Where they differ in kind---accuracy against cost, quality against latency---separating the objective from the constraint set is both more defensible and, on this evidence, more accurate.
