# Assortment Planning and Product Rationalization

Assortment planning decides which products to carry, in what depth
(inventory per SKU), and in what breadth (how many distinct SKUs) — a
decision that sits upstream of forecasting but strongly shapes what "good
forecasting" even means for a given catalog.

**The width/depth tradeoff:** a broad assortment (many SKUs, each thin)
captures more of the market's variety preference but fragments demand
across more items, making each individual SKU's history noisier and
harder to forecast accurately — a narrower assortment concentrates volume
into fewer SKUs, which tends to produce more forecastable, stable demand
per item at the cost of potentially missing some customer segments.

**Rationalization signals** (candidates for reducing or dropping an
item), typically drawn straight from the same rankings/trend data used
elsewhere in demand planning:
- Persistently in the bottom tier of an ABC ranking (low revenue/volume
  share) with no offsetting strategic reason to keep it (loss-leader,
  completes a set, blocks a competitor).
- A declining trend that traces to genuine category/product maturity
  rather than a fixable cause (see sales-trend-interpretation notes) —
  if the decline isn't a stockout, pricing issue, or distribution
  problem, it may reflect real declining demand that rationalization
  should respond to rather than fight.
- High forecast error and high demand variability (Z-classification in
  XYZ terms) combined with low value (C-classification) — these items
  cost disproportionate planning effort for little payoff.

**Adding new items:** a healthy assortment strategy typically pairs
rationalization of weak performers with the introduction of new items to
maintain freshness — using new-product forecasting practices (analog
methods, staged confidence) rather than assuming a new SKU will perform
like the catalog average.

**Practical link to a ranked product-performance view:** a
straightforward "top sellers vs. bottom performers by revenue/share, with
trend direction" report is one of the most directly actionable outputs
for assortment decisions — pair the ranking with *why* an item is
declining (see sales-trend-interpretation and stockout-vs-overstock
notes) before deciding whether the right response is to promote, hold,
markdown, or discontinue it.
